# Talbot County – Health & Public‑Safety Beat  
*Mentor’s Guide for Early‑Career Reporters*  

---  

## 1. Why This Beat Matters  

Talbot County and its five municipalities (Easton, Oxford, Queen Anne’s, St. Michaels, and Trappe) sit at the intersection of a historic Chesapeake Bay community and a fast‑changing public‑policy landscape.  From aging water‑infrastructure and emerging drug‑trafficking concerns to the county’s new police‑accountability framework, the health‑and‑public‑safety beat offers a steady stream of stories that affect every resident’s daily life.  

Your role will be to turn the raw data—council minutes, board reports, police logs—into clear, contextual journalism that helps citizens understand the risks, resources, and decisions shaping their neighborhoods.

## 4. People Profile: Who Shows Up in the Stories  

Below is a narrative overview of the most frequently quoted or featured individuals. Understanding their background will help you anticipate where they may appear next.  

1. **John Bartlett** – As the elected chair of the **Caroline County Police Accountability Board**, Bartlett is the public face of the county’s oversight process. He often frames data in terms of “trust” and “accountability,” and his statements are used by both reform advocates and police unions.  

2. **Kimberly Raeder** – The board’s administrator, Raeder handles the grant‑funding logistics (e.g., the $105 k state grant that covered software for the PAB). She can provide details on budget constraints and the board’s capacity for investigations.  

3. **Lauren Weber & Katie Theeke** – Both hold senior leadership roles at **For All Seasons**, a non‑profit operating across the five Mid‑Shore counties. Weber speaks to strategy (data, trends), while Theeke manages communications (public outreach, training). Their combined perspective gives you a window into how anti‑trafficking work is coordinated regionally.  

4. **Andy Harris** – The district’s U.S. Representative. Though a federal officeholder, his local press conferences (e.g., the March 2025 Dorchester Chamber rally) influence public‑safety discourse, especially around policing and “law‑and‑order” policy.  

5. **Victoria Bradley** – Secretary of the **Talbot County Liquor Board**. She can comment on licensing trends, compliance checks, and the economic ripple effects of alcohol regulation.  

6. **Sharon VanEmburgh** – Town attorney who helped draft the cannabis‑zoning ordinance. She interprets municipal code and can explain how state‑level legislation (House Bill 805) translates into local land‑use decisions.  

7. **Derek Simmons** – Superintendent of Caroline County Public Schools. He is the go‑to source on school‑district policy, especially anything that intersects with public‑health (virtual days, mental‑health services).  

When you meet any of these individuals, ask for “the data that didn’t make the press release” – budget line items, internal memos, or stakeholder lists. Those details often become the backbone of a deeper investigative piece.

**How to Pursue:**  
1. **Start with the public record** (council minutes, board reports, FOIA requests).  
2. **Add human element** – schedule interviews with at least two stakeholders per story (e.g., a board member and a community advocate).  
3. **Cross‑check data** – use state databases (Maryland Open Data Portal) to verify any statistical claim.  
4. **Document the status** – keep a “pending/closed” column in your story‑tracking sheet.

## 7. Practical Tips for Getting Started  

| Action | How to Do It | Why It Helps |
|--------|---------------|--------------|
| **Create a “beat folder”** on your laptop (sub‑folders: *Police Oversight, Water, Health, Education, Crime*). | Store PDFs of council minutes, board reports, and relevant news articles. | Quick reference when you need a baseline. |
| **Set up Google Alerts** for key terms: “Talbot County water,” “Caroline County Police Accountability,” “human trafficking Talbot.” | Alerts arrive daily; you’ll spot new developments before they hit the wire. | Stay ahead of the news cycle. |
| **Build a source list** in a spreadsheet: name, title, email, preferred contact method, last interaction date. | Update after each interview; note any “off‑record” insights you may need to verify later. | Efficient outreach for follow‑ups or story expansions. |
| **Use the county’s open‑data portals** (e.g., Maryland Department of the Environment water‑quality data). | Export CSV files for trend analysis (e.g., lead levels before/after rate hike). | Adds quantitative depth to your reporting. |
| **Attend council and board meetings in person or via livestream** (most are posted on the county website). | Take detailed notes; ask questions during public comment if appropriate. | First‑hand observation of policy debates and community sentiment. |
| **Verify story status before filing** – check if a lawsuit, protest, or investigation has been resolved. | Use the county clerk’s docket, court records, or press releases. | Prevents publishing outdated or inaccurate information. |

## 8. Final Checklist Before Publishing  

- [ ] **Confirm the story’s current status** (ongoing investigation, pending protest, completed project).  
- [ ] **Quote at least two sources** from different stakeholder groups (e.g., official & community).  
- [ ] **Include a data point** (statistics, budget figure, rate increase) and cite the original source.  
- [ ] **Provide context** – link to prior coverage where applicable (use the dataset hyperlinks).  
- [ ] **Check for any legal restrictions** (e.g., minors’ identities, sealed records).  
- [ ] **Proofread for tone** – maintain a professional, mentor‑style voice; avoid colloquialisms.
